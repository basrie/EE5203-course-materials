EE5203 Microprocessors

L13 - Demonstration, Oral Defence, and Reflection

Basri Erdogan

The last question is always the same

Not “does it work?” — by now it either does or it does not. The question is “can you explain it?” Point at any wire, any register, any line, and tell me why it is that way and not another.

Today you demonstrate a system, and then you answer for it individually.

Set the tone early: the defence is not an interrogation, it is the part of the assessment that only the student can do. A team cannot answer for a member.

The story so far

01 L01-L05: you learned to make one thing happen deliberately.

02 L06-L08: several things at once, without blocking.

03 L09-L11: the outside world, in and out.

04 L12: all of it together, with evidence.

05 TODAY: show it, explain it, and say what you would do differently.

Today’s objectives

01 Run a demonstration that survives contact with reality.

02 Present requirements and the evidence that each is met.

03 Defend your own contribution individually, at register level.

04 Report honestly on what did not work and why.

05 Reflect: what you would design differently now.

What is actually assessed

Component What it measures
Demonstration the system meeting its stated requirements, live
Report design decisions, evidence, and honest limitations
Presentation can you explain the system to an engineer who has not seen it
Individual defence what you understand, separately from your team

The first three are team marks. The fourth is not — and it is the one that most often separates two members of the same team.

The demonstration: structure

Fifteen minutes. Rehearse it; do not improvise it.

01 Setup (2 min): power up, show it running before you start talking.

02 Requirements walk (5 min): one requirement, one test, one observed result. In order.

03 Live operation (5 min): the system doing its job, including a fault you inject deliberately.

04 Known issues (3 min): what does not work, and what you know about why.

Demonstrate the failure, too

A system that only works is a demo. A system whose failure you can predict, trigger, and explain is engineering.

Inject a fault on purpose: disconnect the sensor, hold a breakpoint until ORE sets, stall the servo. Then show what the system does about it.

This is the single highest-value thing you can add to a demonstration, and almost nobody does it.

When it fails on the day

It happens. What distinguishes teams is the next thirty seconds.

01 Say what should have happened. You know the expected behaviour; state it.

02 Diagnose out loud. Name the species: component, resource, or timing.

03 Use your instruments. Telemetry, counters, the SFR view — they are still there.

04 Fall back. The tagged commit that worked last week. You did tag it.

A team that diagnoses a live failure convincingly can score higher than one whose demo worked and who cannot say why.

The report

Not a narrative of your term. An engineering document.

01 Requirements — testable sentences, and the result for each.

02 Design decisions — what you chose, what you rejected, and why.

03 Resource map and timing budget — from L12, updated.

04 Evidence — telemetry plots, measurements, screenshots of registers.

05 Known issues and future work — specific, not apologetic.

Design decisions are the marks

“We used TIM3 for the servo” is a fact. This is a decision:

“We put the servo on TIM3 rather than TIM2 because TIM2 was already running the LED at 1 kHz, and channels of one timer share ARR. The servo needs 50 Hz, so it needed its own timer.”

Every such sentence is a mark. Facts are free; reasons are the report.

The individual defence

Five to eight minutes each. Expect questions at three levels:

01 Your contribution: “Which parts did you write? Walk me through one.”

02 The mechanism: “Which register does that configure? What does the hardware do?”

03 The alternative: “Why this way? What would happen if you did it the other way?”

Level 3 is where marks are won. Anyone can describe what they did; explaining why the alternative is worse shows you understood the choice.

Questions you should expect

01 “Show me where you configure that peripheral. Why those values?”

02 “What is in that register right now, and why?”

03 “What would happen if I removed this line?”

04 “How do you know this works? Show me the evidence.”

05 “What is the slowest part of your system, and how do you know?”

06 “What broke during the term, and how did you find it?”

None of these can be prepared as a script. All of them can be prepared by having actually done the work.

“I only did the software”

A common answer, and a costly one.

01 You are asked about your part in depth — but you must be able to place it in the system.

02 Which pins does your code touch? Where do they go physically?

03 What does your part cost in the timing budget?

Division of labour is fine and expected. Not knowing where your own code’s signals go is not.

Reflection: the last mark

Two questions, answered honestly:

01 What would you design differently, knowing what you know now?

02 What cost you the most time, and what would have prevented it?

“Nothing, it went well” scores nothing. Every project has an answer to both, and the specific ones are the most valuable engineering output of the term.

Common demonstration mistakes

01 UNREHEARSED: discovering the projector, the power, or the order live.

02 NO FALLBACK: the current build fails and there is nothing tagged to return to.

03 ONE PERSON KNOWS EVERYTHING: fine for the demo, expensive at the defence.

04 HIDING THE KNOWN ISSUES: they get found anyway, and cost more when they are.

05 NO EVIDENCE: claiming a requirement is met without the measurement.

06 READING THE SLIDES: you built it; talk about it.

07 CHANGING CODE THE MORNING OF: the most reliable way to break a working system.

What good looks like

A system that meets requirements it stated in advance; evidence for each; a fault injected deliberately and explained; an honest known-issues list; and every member able to defend their own part down to the register.

Note what is not on that list: scope, polish, or novelty. A modest system, fully understood and fully evidenced, defends better than an ambitious one that half works.

Mastery checklist

✓ Run a rehearsed 15-minute demonstration with a fallback ready.

✓ State each requirement with its test and observed result.

✓ Inject a fault deliberately and explain the system’s response.

✓ Explain your own contribution at register level.

✓ Justify a design decision against its alternative.

✓ Diagnose a live failure out loud, using your own instruments.

Where this goes next

01 The method transfers. Resource maps, timing budgets, evidence-first debugging — none of that was STM32-specific.

02 The registers transfer. Every microcontroller has a timer with a prescaler, a UART with a divider, and a flag that means lost.

03 The habit transfers. “How do you know?” is the whole of engineering.

Optional extension material — DMA, displays, assembly and disassembly — is available on request if your project or your curiosity needs it.

Exit ticket

Answer for your own contribution, in writing, before you leave.

01 PART: Which parts of the system did you write, and which resources do they own?

02 MECHANISM: Name one register your code configures and say what the hardware does with it.

03 DECISION: State one design choice you made and why the alternative was worse.

04 EVIDENCE: Which requirement does your part satisfy, and how is it tested?

05 REFLECTION: What cost you the most time, and what would have prevented it?
