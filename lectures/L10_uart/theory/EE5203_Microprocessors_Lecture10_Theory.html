The number nobody can see

Last lecture you acquired a real voltage and converted it to 1650. That number existed for a few microseconds inside a register, drove an LED, and vanished. Nobody watched it. Nothing recorded it.

Today the number leaves the board: onto your screen, into a log file, into a plot. And a command comes back the other way.

Open by asking how they have debugged so far: breakpoints and the Watch panel. Both stop the program. Point out that you cannot breakpoint a motor mid-flight.

L09 recap: acquired, then lost

uint16_t raw = adc_read(ADC_CHANNEL_0);
uint32_t mv  = (raw * 3300u) / 4095u;      /* 1650 - and then? */

01 A breakpoint shows you one value, once, with the program stopped.

02 The Watch panel shows the current value — but you cannot watch a trend.

03 Neither works on a system that must keep running: a motor, a flight, a furnace.

Debugging by stopping time only works when stopping time is allowed.

Why serial?

Parallel Serial
Wires for 8 bits 8 + ground 1 + ground
Skew between bits must be managed impossible — there is one line
Cost at distance grows fast stays low
Speed per wire lower clock higher clock

We trade time for wires: one line, one bit at a time, agreed in advance how fast.

Asynchronous: no clock wire

Synchronous links (SPI, I2C — next lecture) carry a clock line: the receiver is told when to look.

A UART has no clock line. Instead both ends agree beforehand on the bit rate, and the receiver re-synchronises on every frame using the start bit.

01 The line idles high.

02 A falling edge — the start bit — says “a frame begins now.”

03 The receiver then samples at pre-agreed intervals, in the middle of each bit.

The frame, on the wire

What 8N1 means

8 data bits per frame, sent least significant first.

N no parity bit. (E = even, O = odd.)

1 one stop bit — the line returns high for at least one bit time.

So one byte costs 10 bit times: 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop. Parity would make it 11.

Both ends must agree on all four things — baud, data bits, parity, stop bits. Agreeing on three out of four produces garbage, not a warning.

The numbers

\[\text{time per byte} = \frac{10}{\text{baud}} \qquad \text{bytes per second} = \frac{\text{baud}}{10}\]

Baud µs per byte bytes/s
9 600 1041.67 960
115 200 86.81 11 520

“115200 baud” means 115 200 bits per second on the wire — not bytes. The factor of 10 is the framing overhead you just drew.

Practice: how long is a line?

Your telemetry line is raw=2048 mv=1650\r\n — 18 characters — sent at 115 200 baud.

1 How long does one line take to transmit?

2 At 10 lines per second, what fraction of the link are you using?

3 What is the fastest you could send this line, ignoring everything else?

18 × 10 / 115200 = 1.56 ms. At 10 Hz that is 1.6 % of the link. Flat out: 640 lines/s. Telemetry is cheap — until you print inside a 1 kHz loop.

Baud mismatch

The receiver samples at its bit rate, not yours. Get the rate wrong and it samples the right wire at the wrong moments.

01 Consistent garbage (same wrong characters for the same input) → baud mismatch.

02 Nothing at all → wrong port, wrong pin, or TX/RX not crossed.

03 First character wrong, rest fine → the receiver was opened mid-frame.

There is no handshake and no error message. A UART cannot tell you that you disagree — it can only hand you the disagreement.

You already have the wire

The Nucleo’s ST-LINK chip is also a USB-to-serial bridge. PA2 and PA3 are wired to it on the board.

01 PA2 = USART2_TX → ST-LINK → your PC.

02 PA3 = USART2_RX ← ST-LINK ← your PC.

03 On the PC it appears as a serial port. VS Code’s Serial Monitor opens it.

No jumpers, no USB-TTL dongle, no level shifter. The cable that flashes the board also carries your data.

CubeMX: USART2

01 Connectivity → USART2: Mode = Asynchronous.

02 Baud 115200, Word Length 8 Bits, Parity None, Stop 1 — that is 8N1.

03 PA2 and PA3 turn green automatically: alternate function AF7.

04 NVIC: enable USART2 global interrupt — needed for receiving.

The generated init

huart2.Instance          = USART2;
huart2.Init.BaudRate     = 115200;
huart2.Init.WordLength   = UART_WORDLENGTH_8B;
huart2.Init.StopBits     = UART_STOPBITS_1;
huart2.Init.Parity       = UART_PARITY_NONE;
huart2.Init.Mode         = UART_MODE_TX_RX;   /* both directions */
huart2.Init.OverSampling = UART_OVERSAMPLING_16;
HAL_UART_Init(&huart2);

HAL_UART_Init computes and writes BRR. Everything above is a request; BRR is what the hardware actually does.

BRR: how the baud is made

USART2 sits on APB1. At the default HSI clock, PCLK1 = 16 MHz. With oversampling by 16:

\[\text{USARTDIV} = \frac{f_{\text{PCLK}}}{16 \times \text{baud}}\]

The result is stored as a fixed-point number: the top 12 bits of BRR are the integer part, the bottom 4 bits are sixteenths.

For 115 200 baud: USARTDIV = 16 000 000 / (16 × 115 200) = 8.6806 → mantissa 8, fraction 0.6806 × 16 ≈ 11 → BRR = (8 << 4) | 11 = 139 = 0x08B

The baud you asked for is not the baud you get

Actual rate = 16 000 000 / (16 × 8.6875) = 115 108 baud — an error of −0.08 %.

Baud BRR Actual Error
9 600 0x683 9 598 −0.02 %
38 400 0x1A1 38 369 −0.08 %
115 200 0x08B 115 108 −0.08 %
230 400 0x045 231 884 +0.64 %

A UART tolerates roughly ±2 % total error across both ends. 0.08 % is invisible; 0.64 % eats a third of the budget before the other device has said anything.

Practice: compute BRR

PCLK1 = 16 MHz, oversampling by 16.

1 Compute USARTDIV for 9 600 baud.

2 Split it into mantissa and fraction, and give BRR in hex.

3 What actual baud results, and what is the error?

16e6/(16×9600) = 104.1667 → mantissa 104, fraction 0.1667×16 ≈ 3 → BRR = (104<<4)|3 = 1667 = 0x683. Actual 9598.1 baud, −0.02 %.

Transmitting: two flags

TXE Transmit data register empty — DR is free, you may write the next byte. The previous one may still be going out.

TC Transmission complete — the shift register is empty too. The line is genuinely idle.

TXE means “give me more.” TC means “everything has left the building.” Powering down or switching a transceiver direction needs TC, not TXE.

HAL_UART_Transmit blocks

HAL_UART_Transmit(&huart2, (uint8_t *)buf, len, 100);

The last argument is a timeout in milliseconds. The call spins until every byte has been handed to the hardware.

01 An 18-character line at 115 200 baud blocks for 1.56 ms.

02 That is one and a half L07 tick periods, spent waiting.

03 Acceptable at 10 Hz from main(). Never acceptable inside a callback.

Building a line

char    buf[48];
uint16_t raw = adc_read(ADC_CHANNEL_0);
uint32_t mv  = (raw * 3300u) / 4095u;

int n = snprintf(buf, sizeof(buf), "raw=%u mv=%lu\r\n",
                 (unsigned)raw, (unsigned long)mv);

HAL_UART_Transmit(&huart2, (uint8_t *)buf, (uint16_t)n, 100);

01 snprintf cannot overflow buf — it truncates instead. sprintf can and will.

02 It returns the length it wanted; clamp before using it if truncation is possible.

03 \r\n — most serial monitors expect both.

Telemetry from the tick

if ((g_ms - t_tx) >= 100U) {          /* 10 Hz, scheduled by L07 */
    t_tx = g_ms;
    uint16_t raw = adc_read(ADC_CHANNEL_0);
    int n = snprintf(buf, sizeof buf, "%lu,%u\r\n",
                     (unsigned long)g_ms, (unsigned)raw);
    HAL_UART_Transmit(&huart2, (uint8_t *)buf, (uint16_t)n, 100);
}

Comma-separated with a timestamp: paste it straight into a spreadsheet and plot it. Telemetry you cannot plot is just scrolling.

Receiving: RXNE

RXNE Read data register not empty — a complete frame has arrived and DR holds the byte.

02 Reading DR clears RXNE — the same read-to-clear idea as the ADC’s EOC.

ORE Overrun — a new byte arrived before you read the previous one. That byte is gone.

Blocking receive is a trap

HAL_UART_Receive(&huart2, &c, 1, HAL_MAX_DELAY);   /* DO NOT */

This waits for a keystroke. Meanwhile your servo does not move, your tick does not tick, and your project appears to have crashed — because it has, from the outside.

Input arrives when a human decides, not when your loop is ready. That is exactly the situation interrupts exist for — the L06 lesson, one more source.

Receive by interrupt

uint8_t rx_byte;

/* arm it once, after HAL_UART_Init */
HAL_UART_Receive_IT(&huart2, &rx_byte, 1);

void HAL_UART_RxCpltCallback(UART_HandleTypeDef *huart)
{
    if (huart->Instance == USART2) {
        rx_ring[head] = rx_byte;          /* record - nothing else */
        head = (head + 1) % RX_SIZE;
        HAL_UART_Receive_IT(&huart2, &rx_byte, 1);   /* re-arm! */
    }
}

Re-arming is mandatory. HAL disarms after each completed receive. Forget the re-arm and you get exactly one byte, ever — then silence that looks like broken hardware.

Assembling a command

/* in main(): drain what the callback recorded */
while (tail != head) {
    char c = rx_ring[tail];
    tail = (tail + 1) % RX_SIZE;

    if (c == '\r' || c == '\n') {
        line[len] = '\0';
        handle_command(line);           /* act in main, not in the ISR */
        len = 0;
    } else if (len < sizeof(line) - 1) {
        line[len++] = c;                /* bounds check: always */
    }
}

The callback records bytes; main() decides what they mean. Same discipline as every interrupt since L06.

A tiny command set

void handle_command(const char *s)
{
    if      (strcmp(s, "led on")  == 0) { led_mode = 1; }
    else if (strcmp(s, "led off") == 0) { led_mode = 0; }
    else if (strncmp(s, "duty ", 5) == 0) {
        int d = atoi(s + 5);
        if (d >= 0 && d <= 999) { __HAL_TIM_SET_COMPARE(&htim2, TIM_CHANNEL_1, d); }
        else { reply("range 0..999\r\n"); }
    }
    else { reply("? unknown\r\n"); }
}

Always reply — including to a bad command. A silent device is indistinguishable from a dead one.

Overrun, and why it happens

ORE sets when a byte arrives before you read the previous one. Causes, in order of likelihood:

01 Blocking somewhere long — a HAL_Delay, a slow Transmit, a breakpoint.

02 Receive interrupt never re-armed.

03 Baud far higher than the loop can drain.

HAL will not deliver further bytes until the error is cleared. Silent, permanent, and easily mistaken for a hardware fault.

Making printf work

int __io_putchar(int ch)                 /* newlib calls this per character */
{
    HAL_UART_Transmit(&huart2, (uint8_t *)&ch, 1, 10);
    return ch;
}

01 Convenient — and expensive: printf with floats pulls in a large library and a lot of stack.

02 One HAL_UART_Transmit per character is far slower than one per line.

03 Fine for a banner at startup; wrong for a 10 Hz telemetry stream.

The USART registers

Register Role
USART2->BRR the baud divider, mantissa:fraction
USART2->CR1 UE enable, TE/RE transmit/receive enable, RXNEIE interrupt enable, M word length, PCE/PS parity
USART2->CR2 STOP bits
USART2->SR TXE, TC, RXNE, ORE
USART2->DR data — write to send, read to receive

One DR for both directions. Writing it loads the transmit shift register; reading it takes from the receive one. They are physically different registers behind one address.

Register evidence

01 USART2->BRR reads 0x08B for 115 200 at 16 MHz — check it against your own arithmetic.

02 GPIOA->MODER bits for PA2/PA3 read 10, and AFR[0] selects AF7.

03 Pause with a character pending: SR shows RXNE = 1; step over the read and it clears.

04 Hold a breakpoint while typing fast, then resume: ORE is set — you lost bytes and the hardware says so.

A successful build proves none of this. The SFR view does.

Common UART mistakes

01 BAUD MISMATCH: monitor at 9600, board at 115200. Consistent garbage.

02 RECEIVE NEVER RE-ARMED: exactly one byte received, then permanent silence.

03 sprintf INTO A SMALL BUFFER: silent stack corruption. Use snprintf.

04 TRANSMIT INSIDE THE CALLBACK: 1.5 ms of blocking in interrupt context.

05 ORE NEVER CLEARED: reception stops for good, looks like dead hardware.

06 \n WITHOUT \r: the monitor stair-steps instead of returning to column 1.

07 MONITOR STILL OPEN WHILE FLASHING: the port is busy and the flash fails.

Laboratory mission

Make the board talk and listen. Stream timestamped ADC readings at 10 Hz over the ST-LINK virtual COM port, then accept typed commands that change the LED duty — with the tick, the PWM, and the ADC all still running underneath.

You will show:

  1. BRR matching your own hand-computed value;
  2. telemetry at a measured 10 Hz, non-blocking, pasteable into a spreadsheet;
  3. interrupt-driven receive with a correct re-arm;
  4. a command that acts, and a bad command that is refused politely;
  5. a deliberate ORE, observed in SR and explained.
