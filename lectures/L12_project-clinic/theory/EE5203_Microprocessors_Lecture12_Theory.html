L12 - System Integration and Project Clinic

Basri Erdogan

Every part works. The system does not.

Your timer works. Your ADC works. Your UART works. You put all three in one program and the servo stutters, the display flickers, and one reading in twenty is nonsense. Nothing you tested is broken.

Integration failures are a different species from component failures, and they need a different method.

Ask for a show of hands: who has had something work alone and break when combined? This is the lecture where that stops being bad luck.

Three species of failure

Species Symptom Where it hides
Component one thing is wrong, always in your code, reproducibly
Resource conflict two things fight over one thing in the .ioc, silently
Timing works most of the time in the schedule, intermittently

You have spent eleven weeks learning to find species one. Species two and three are what this lecture is about — and they are what actually breaks demonstrations.

Resource conflicts: the map

Before debugging anything, write down what owns what.

Resource Owner Used for
TIM2 CH1 / PA5 PWM LED brightness
TIM3 CH1 / PA6 PWM servo
TIM4 CH1/2 / PB6 input capture measurement
ADC1 IN0 / PA0 ADC knob
USART2 / PA2, PA3 UART telemetry + commands
I2C1 / PB8, PB9 I2C display
SysTick HAL HAL_GetTick, HAL_Delay

Most integration bugs are visible in this table before they are visible on the bench. Two owners in one row is a bug you have not met yet.

The conflicts that actually happen

01 Same pin, two functions. PA5 is LD2 and TIM2_CH1 and SPI1_SCK. CubeMX will let you change your mind and leave the old init behind.

02 Same timer, two frequencies. Channels share ARR. The 1 kHz LED and the 50 Hz servo cannot cohabit.

03 HAL_Delay inside an interrupt. It depends on SysTick; at equal or higher priority it never returns.

04 One ADC, two channels, one rank. Reconfigured per read — so the two samples are not simultaneous.

Build a timing budget

Add up what must happen each second. Anything over 100 % is a design error, not a bug.

Activity Cost Rate Load
Tick interrupt ~2 µs 1000 Hz 0.2 %
ADC read ~20 µs 100 Hz 0.2 %
UART line (blocking) 1.56 ms 10 Hz 1.6 %
LCD line (I2C, 100 kHz) 7.2 ms 2 Hz 1.4 %
Total 3.4 %

Now raise the LCD to 10 Hz: 72 ms per second, 7.2 % — five times the whole rest of the system. The ceiling is about 139 Hz, where the display alone consumes 100 % and nothing else ever runs.

Practice: does it fit?

A team proposes: 1 ms tick; ADC read every 10 ms; a 20-character UART line at 50 Hz; a full 16-character LCD refresh at 20 Hz; I2C at 100 kHz.

1 Compute the load of the UART and the LCD separately.

2 Does the system fit? Which item dominates?

3 Give the cheapest single change that brings it under 20 %.

UART: 20 × 10 / 115200 = 1.74 ms × 50 Hz = 8.7 %. LCD: 7.2 ms × 20 Hz = 14.4 %. Total ≈ 23 % — it fits, but the display alone costs more than everything else combined. Cheapest fix: drop the LCD to 2 Hz (1.4 %), taking the total to about 10 %.

Interrupt discipline, restated

Every rule from L06 onward, in one place:

01 A callback records; main() decides.

02 No HAL_Delay, no blocking transmit, no loops of unknown length.

03 Shared variables are volatile; multi-byte ones need care when read.

04 A callback longer than its own period is a design that cannot work.

Nearly every “random” integration failure traces back to one of these four.

Debugging a system you cannot stop

A breakpoint stops the CPU. It does not stop the timer, the motor, the sensor, or the person waiting for your demo.

01 Telemetry (L10) is your instrument. Log state, not prose.

02 A GPIO pin toggled around a section costs nanoseconds and shows timing on any scope — or on an input-capture channel you already know how to use.

03 Counters for events you cannot watch: overruns, NACKs, missed deadlines. Print them once a second.

“It hangs sometimes” is not a bug report. “It hangs after 400–600 s, and ore_count is non-zero when it does” is one.

Bisection beats guessing

When a system misbehaves and you do not know why, halve it.

01 Comment out half the subsystems. Does the fault survive?

02 Yes → the fault is in the remaining half. No → it is in what you removed, or in the interaction.

03 Repeat. Six halvings isolate one line out of sixty.

04 Same method on time: git bisect between the last known-good commit and now.

Guessing scales linearly with the size of the program. Bisection scales logarithmically. That is the whole argument.

“It worked yesterday”

01 Commit before every change you might want to undo.

02 Commit the .ioc with the generated code — regeneration is a code change.

03 Tag the version you demonstrated. You will be asked to run it again.

A team that cannot return to the version that worked last week has lost more than the code — they have lost the evidence that it ever did.

The physical layer bites last

Software-only teams meet these for the first time at integration:

01 Grounds must be common. Two supplies, no shared ground — nothing works and nothing says why.

02 Current budget. A servo stalling pulls an amp. The ST-LINK’s 3.3 V rail supplies a few hundred milliamps.

03 Brownout. The board resets mid-demo and comes back clean, so the log shows a fresh start and no error.

04 Loose jumpers. Intermittent, position-dependent, and indistinguishable from a software race.

Practice: name the species

1 The display works until the servo sweeps, then shows garbage.

2 Telemetry is perfect for ten minutes, then stops permanently.

3 The LED dims correctly but the servo never moves, from the first run.

  1. Physical — servo current browning out the rail, or ground noise on I2C. (2) Timing/resource — very likely ORE set and never cleared. (3) Component — a plain configuration error, reproducible from the start.

What a readiness review needs

Not “it works.” Evidence that it works:

01 A requirement list — what the system must do, in testable sentences.

02 A test for each requirement, with the result you actually observed.

03 A resource map and a timing budget that add up.

04 A known-issues list. Every real system has one; hiding it costs more marks than having it.

Writing a testable requirement

Not testable Testable
“The display is fast” “The display updates within 500 ms of a knob change”
“It reads temperature” “Reported temperature is within ±2 °C of a reference between 10 and 40 °C”
“It is reliable” “Runs 30 minutes with no reset and no lost telemetry lines”

If you cannot describe the measurement, you cannot claim the requirement — and the defence in L13 will ask you to run it.

The clinic: how today’s session runs

01 Ten minutes per team. Show the system in its current state.

02 Present your resource map and timing budget — these are the checkoff.

03 Name your top three risks and what you will do about each.

04 Leave with a written list of what must be true by L13.

This is the last session before the demonstration. Bring the problem you are most afraid of, not the part you are proudest of.

Common integration mistakes

01 NO RESOURCE MAP: two owners for one timer, discovered at the demo.

02 NO TIMING BUDGET: a display refresh that cannot fit in a second.

03 BLOCKING IN A CALLBACK: everything else starves.

04 DEBUGGING BY GUESSING: changing three things at once, then not knowing which helped.

05 NO COMMITS: cannot return to the version that worked.

06 SHARED GROUND FORGOTTEN: hours lost to a wire that was never there.

07 INTEGRATING IN WEEK 14: the single most reliable way to fail this course.

Laboratory mission

Bring the whole system to the bench and prove it holds together: a resource map with no conflicts, a timing budget that fits, one fault found by bisection rather than guesswork, and a requirement list with a test beside each line.

You will show:

  1. the resource map, complete and conflict-free;
  2. the timing budget, summing to a stated percentage;
  3. a 10-minute continuous run with telemetry and no resets;
  4. one fault diagnosed by bisection, with the steps recorded;
  5. requirements, tests, results, and an honest known-issues list.

Mastery checklist

✓ Build a resource map and spot a conflict in it.

Exit ticket

A team runs a 1 ms tick, reads two ADC channels every 5 ms (20 µs each), sends a 24-character UART line at 115 200 baud every 100 ms, and refreshes a 16-character LCD over 100 kHz I2C every 250 ms.

01 BUDGET: Compute the load of each activity and the total.

02 DOMINANT: Which item costs the most, and by what factor over the next?

03 SPECIES: Their servo jitters only while the LCD updates. Which species of failure, and why?

04 METHOD: They have four subsystems and one intermittent fault. How many bisection steps to isolate it?

05 EVIDENCE: Rewrite “the system is responsive” as a testable requirement.
