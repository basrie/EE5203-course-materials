EE5203 L12 Study Sheet

System integration and the project clinic - Basri Erdogan

Essential question: Every part works alone and the system does not. What kind of failure is that, and what method finds it — given that you cannot stop the program to look?

What you should be able to do

  • Build a resource map and spot a conflict in it before it reaches the bench.
  • Compute a timing budget and judge whether a design fits.
  • Distinguish component, resource, and timing failures by symptom.
  • Instrument a running system with telemetry, a GPIO pin, and counters.
  • Bisect a fault in code and in history.
  • Write a requirement that can actually be tested.

Three species of failure

Species Symptom Found by
Component one thing wrong, always, from the first run ordinary debugging
Resource conflict two subsystems fight over one resource the resource map
Timing works most of the time; fails under load the timing budget

The species tells you which tool to reach for. Reaching for a breakpoint on a timing failure is how afternoons disappear.

The resource map

One row per resource, exactly one owner each.

Resource Pin(s) Owner
TIM2 CH1 PA5 LED PWM
TIM3 CH1 PA6 servo PWM
TIM4 CH1/2 PB6 input capture
ADC1 IN0/IN1 PA0, PA1 knob, light sensor
USART2 PA2, PA3 telemetry + commands
I2C1 PB8, PB9 display
SysTick — HAL, HAL_GetTick

The four conflicts that actually happen:

  1. One pin, two functions. PA5 is LD2, TIM2_CH1, and SPI1_SCK. Changing your mind in CubeMX can leave the old init behind.
  2. One timer, two frequencies. Channels share PSC and ARR. A 1 kHz LED and a 50 Hz servo cannot share a timer.
  3. HAL_Delay reachable from an interrupt. It waits on SysTick; at equal or higher priority it never returns.
  4. Two ADC channels assumed simultaneous. One converter, one multiplexer — the samples are separated by at least a conversion time.

The timing budget

\[\text{load} = \frac{\text{cost per occurrence} \times \text{rate}}{1\ \text{second}}\]

Activity Cost Rate Load
Tick interrupt ~2 µs 1000 Hz 0.2 %
ADC read ~20 µs 100 Hz 0.2 %
UART line, 18 ch @ 115 200 1.56 ms 10 Hz 1.6 %
LCD line, 16 ch @ 100 kHz 7.2 ms 2 Hz 1.4 %

Useful reference figures from earlier lectures:

  • UART: \(\text{chars} \times 10 / \text{baud}\) seconds per line.
  • I2C LCD: 45 bits per character (4 data bytes + address) → 450 µs at 100 kHz, 112 µs at 400 kHz.
  • Above ~50 % total, you need a plan. Above 100 %, it is a design error.

Measure rather than guess: toggle a spare GPIO around a section and capture it — on a scope, or on the input-capture channel you built in L08.

Interrupt discipline, in one place

  1. A callback records; main() decides.
  2. No HAL_Delay, no blocking transmit, no loop of unknown length.
  3. Shared variables are volatile.
  4. A callback longer than its own period is a design that cannot work.

Nearly every “random” integration failure traces to one of these four.

Debugging what you cannot stop

Instrument Cost Shows
Telemetry (L10) ~1.6 ms/line state over time, plottable
GPIO toggle nanoseconds exact timing of a section
Event counters negligible overruns, NACKs, missed deadlines

Print counters once a second. “It hangs sometimes” is not a bug report; “it hangs after 400–600 s and ore_count is non-zero when it does” is one.

Bisection

When you do not know why, halve it:

  1. Disable half the subsystems. Does the fault survive?
  2. Yes → it is in the remaining half. No → in what you removed, or the interaction between the two.
  3. Repeat. Six halvings isolate one item out of sixty.

The same method applies to time: git bisect between the last known-good commit and now. Guessing scales linearly with program size; bisection scales logarithmically.

The physical layer

  • Common ground between every supply. Missing it fails silently.
  • Current budget. A stalling servo draws an amp; the ST-LINK’s 3.3 V rail supplies a few hundred milliamps.
  • Brownout. The board resets and comes back clean, so the log shows a fresh start and no error at all.
  • Loose jumpers. Intermittent and position-dependent — indistinguishable from a software race until you wiggle them.

Testable requirements

Not testable Testable
“The display is fast” “The display updates within 500 ms of a knob change”
“It reads temperature” “Within ±2 °C of a reference between 10 and 40 °C”
“It is reliable” “Runs 30 minutes with no reset and no lost telemetry lines”

If you cannot describe the measurement, you cannot claim the requirement — and the L13 defence will ask you to run it.

Common mistakes

  • No resource map; the conflict is found at the demonstration.
  • No timing budget; a refresh rate that cannot fit in a second.
  • Blocking inside a callback.
  • Changing three things at once, then not knowing which helped.
  • No commits, so the version that worked is unrecoverable.
  • Shared ground forgotten.
  • Leaving integration to week 14.

Mastery self-check

Five review questions

  1. A team runs a 1 ms tick, a 20-character UART line at 20 Hz, and a 16-character LCD refresh at 5 Hz over 100 kHz I2C. Compute each load and the total. Which dominates?
  2. The servo jitters only while the display updates. Name the two most likely species and the test that distinguishes them.
  3. A system has six subsystems and one intermittent fault. How many bisection steps are needed in the worst case? Show the reasoning.
  4. Rewrite as testable requirements: “the logging is reliable” and “the motor responds quickly.”
  5. Telemetry runs perfectly for eight minutes and then stops for good, while the LED keeps breathing. Give the most likely cause, the counter you would add to confirm it, and the fix.
