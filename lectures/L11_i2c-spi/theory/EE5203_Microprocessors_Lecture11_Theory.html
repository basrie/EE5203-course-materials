Two wires, eight devices

A display, a temperature sensor, a real-time clock, an accelerometer. Four chips that have never met, sharing two wires — and every one of them knows when it is being spoken to.

Last lecture’s UART connected exactly two ends and needed no clock. Today we add a clock line, and in exchange we get addressing, acknowledgement, and a bus.

Hold up the LCD module. Point out it has four pins: power, ground, and two signal wires. Ask how a chip knows a message is meant for it.

L10 recap: agreement without evidence

A UART transmits into the dark. If the other end is absent, misconfigured, or asleep, the sender learns nothing.

01 No clock line — both ends assume a rate.

02 No addressing — the wire goes to exactly one place.

03 No acknowledgement — nothing ever confirms receipt.

Today all three change. The clock removes the assumption, the address removes the one-to-one restriction, and the ACK removes the silence.

Adding a clock wire

In a synchronous bus, one device drives a clock line. Every bit is valid at a defined edge of that clock.

01 No baud agreement needed — the receiver is told when to look.

02 The master sets the pace, and may pause between bytes with no penalty.

03 Cost: one more wire, and one clear master.

A UART frame must be delivered at a fixed rate once started. A synchronous transfer can stop mid-byte and resume a second later — the clock simply waits.

I2C: two wires, many devices

Wire Name Driven by
SCL serial clock the master
SDA serial data master and slave, at different times

Every device on the bus has a 7-bit address. All of them see every transaction; only the addressed one responds.

Two wires, up to 127 addresses, one master in this course. That is the trade I2C makes: fewer wires, more protocol.

Open drain, and why pull-ups

Both I2C lines are open drain: a device can only pull a line low. Nothing drives it high.

01 External pull-up resistors (typically 4.7 kΩ) return each line to VDD.

02 Any device may pull low at any time — so two devices talking at once cannot short each other.

03 This is what makes a shared bidirectional wire safe.

No pull-ups, no bus. The lines sit low, no START is ever seen, and every transfer times out. Most breakout boards include them — which is why forgetting them fails only sometimes.

The I2C transaction

Addressing, and HAL’s shift

The address is 7 bits, followed by one R/W bit — together they fill one byte on the wire.

01 Datasheets quote the 7-bit address: the LCD backpack is 0x27.

02 STM32 HAL wants the 8-bit form, already shifted: 0x27 << 1 = 0x4E.

03 Pass 0x27 to HAL and you address device 0x13 — which is not there.

This single off-by-one-shift costs more lab hours than any other I2C detail. If a device will not answer, check the shift before the wiring.

ACK: the device answers

After every byte, the receiver pulls SDA low for one clock — an acknowledge.

ACK line pulled low → “I am here, and I took that byte.”

NACK line left high → nobody at that address, or no more data wanted.

This is what a UART cannot do. HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit returning HAL_ERROR on a NACK is the bus telling you the truth: nothing answered.

Practice: what is on the bus?

You send address byte 0x4E, then 0x00, and the analyser shows the slave pulling SDA low after each.

1 What 7-bit address did you actually address, and was it a read or a write?

2 What do the two low pulses mean?

3 The same code with 0x27 instead of 0x4E returns HAL_ERROR. Why?

0x4E = 0100 1110: address 0x27, R/W = 0 → write. Two ACKs: the device exists and accepted both bytes. With 0x27 you address 0x13, nobody answers, NACK → HAL_ERROR.

Speed, and what it costs

Mode Clock One byte + ACK
Standard 100 kHz 90 µs
Fast 400 kHz 22.5 µs

The LCD backpack is a port expander: each character needs four I2C bytes (two nibbles, each with the enable line strobed high then low).

One character ≈ 45 bits ≈ 450 µs at 100 kHz. A full 16-character line ≈ 7.2 ms — more than seven L07 tick periods. Refresh the whole display at 10 Hz and you have spent 7 % of your CPU on a screen nobody reads that fast.

I2C in CubeMX

01 Connectivity → I2C1: Mode I2C; PB8 = SCL, PB9 = SDA (AF4).

02 Speed 100 kHz to start — raise it only once it works.

03 The pins become open drain automatically. Check OTYPER.

04 Pull-ups: on the breakout board. Internal pull-ups are too weak for a real bus.

HAL I2C, and finding what is there

/* Is anybody home? Sweep every address and report the ACKs. */
for (uint8_t a = 1; a < 128; a++) {
    if (HAL_I2C_IsDeviceReady(&hi2c1, (uint16_t)(a << 1), 2, 5) == HAL_OK) {
        /* report the 7-bit address 'a' over UART - L10's stream, reused */
    }
}

HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit(&hi2c1, 0x4E, data, len, 100);
HAL_I2C_Mem_Read(&hi2c1, 0xD0, reg, I2C_MEMADD_SIZE_8BIT, buf, n, 100);

A bus scan is the first thing to run and the last thing students think of. It answers “is it wired, powered, and at the address I believe?” in one step.

SPI: four wires, one master

Wire Name Direction
SCK serial clock master → slave
MOSI master out, slave in master → slave
MISO master in, slave out slave → master
CS/NSS chip select, active low master → one slave

No addresses. Selection is physical: one dedicated CS line per device.

Three shared wires plus one select line per device. Two devices: five wires. Eight devices: eleven. That is SPI’s real cost, and it is the reason I2C exists.

Full duplex: an exchange, not a send

Every SPI transfer is a swap. The master and slave shift registers are wired into one ring: for every bit clocked out on MOSI, a bit arrives on MISO.

uint8_t tx = 0xA5, rx = 0;
HAL_SPI_TransmitReceive(&hspi2, &tx, &rx, 1, 100);

01 To read, you must still write something — usually a dummy byte.

02 There is no ACK and no addressing. The wire is trusted.

03 Nothing tells you a slave is absent: reads come back 0x00 or 0xFF.

CPOL and CPHA

Two bits decide when data is valid, and both ends must agree.

CPOL the idle level of the clock: 0 = idles low, 1 = idles high.

CPHA which clock edge samples the data: 0 = the leading edge, 1 = the trailing edge.

Get these wrong and you do not get silence — you get plausible garbage, often shifted by one bit position. That is far harder to spot than nothing at all.

The four SPI modes

Mode CPOL CPHA Clock idles Data sampled on
0 0 0 low leading (rising) edge
1 0 1 low trailing (falling) edge
2 1 0 high leading (falling) edge
3 1 1 high trailing (rising) edge

Mode 0 is by far the most common. The datasheet of the slave decides; the master obeys.

Practice: read the timing diagram

A datasheet shows the clock idling high, with each data bit becoming valid on the falling edge and sampled on the rising edge.

1 What are CPOL and CPHA?

2 Which mode number is that?

3 You configure mode 0 instead. What do you observe?

Idles high → CPOL = 1. Sampled on the trailing (rising) edge → CPHA = 1. That is mode 3. With mode 0 the master samples on the wrong edge and reads each bit half a period early — consistent, shifted nonsense rather than silence.

Chip select is yours to drive

HAL_GPIO_WritePin(CS_PORT, CS_PIN, GPIO_PIN_RESET);   /* select   */
HAL_SPI_TransmitReceive(&hspi2, tx, rx, n, 100);
HAL_GPIO_WritePin(CS_PORT, CS_PIN, GPIO_PIN_SET);     /* deselect */

01 CS is an ordinary GPIO output — HAL will not toggle it for you.

02 It must stay low for the whole multi-byte transaction.

03 Releasing it between bytes resets most slaves’ internal state machines.

Loopback: proving the shift register

Connect MOSI to MISO with one jumper. The bus now talks to itself.

uint8_t tx = 0xA5, rx = 0;
HAL_SPI_TransmitReceive(&hspi2, &tx, &rx, 1, 100);
/* rx == 0xA5  ->  clock, pins and shift register all work */

The cheapest diagnostic in the course: it separates “my SPI is misconfigured” from “the slave is not answering,” and needs no slave at all.

Choosing between them

I2C SPI
Wires 2 + pull-ups 3 + one CS per device
Addressing 7-bit, in protocol physical, one line each
Acknowledgement yes — the device confirms none
Typical speed 100–400 kHz 1–50 MHz
Many devices cheap expensive in pins
Detects a missing device yes, NACK no

Many slow devices, few pins → I2C. One fast device, pins to spare → SPI. Sensors and character displays tend to I2C; SD cards, flash and pixel displays tend to SPI.

The registers

Register Role
I2C1->CR1 PE enable, START, STOP, ACK
I2C1->SR1/SR2 SB start sent, ADDR matched, TXE, RXNE, AF acknowledge failure
I2C1->CCR clock control — sets 100 or 400 kHz
SPI2->CR1 SPE enable, MSTR master, CPOL, CPHA, BR baud divider
SPI2->SR TXE, RXNE, BSY, OVR
SPI2->DR data — one register, both directions

Deep dive: RM0368 Rev 6, I2C — p. 476, SPI — p. 550.

Register evidence

01 GPIOB->OTYPER bits 8 and 9 read 1 — open drain, as I2C requires.

02 A NACK sets AF in I2C1->SR1. Address a device that is not there and watch it appear.

03 SPI2->CR1 shows your CPOL/CPHA — compare against the slave’s datasheet, not against what you meant to set.

04 In loopback, SPI2->DR returns exactly what you wrote.

A successful build proves none of this. The SFR view does.

Common mistakes

01 THE 7-BIT/8-BIT SHIFT: passing 0x27 where HAL wants 0x4E.

02 NO PULL-UPS: lines stuck low, every transfer times out.

03 SDA AND SCL SWAPPED: nothing answers; a bus scan finds nothing at all.

04 IGNORING THE RETURN VALUE: HAL_ERROR on a NACK is the bus telling you the truth.

05 WRONG SPI MODE: plausible, bit-shifted garbage rather than silence.

06 CS RELEASED MID-TRANSACTION: the slave’s state machine resets between bytes.

07 EXPECTING SPI TO REPORT A MISSING SLAVE: it cannot. Reads return 0x00 or 0xFF.

08 REFRESHING THE WHOLE LCD EVERY TICK: 7.2 ms per line, spent on a display nobody reads that fast.

Laboratory mission

Put a real device on a real bus. Scan I2C to discover the LCD’s address, drive it to show live ADC readings, and prove an SPI link works with a single loopback jumper — then read both buses’ registers as evidence.

You will show:

  1. a bus scan that finds the LCD and reports its 7-bit address;
  2. the display showing live millivolts, refreshed at a justified rate;
  3. AF set deliberately by addressing a device that is not there;
  4. SPI loopback returning the byte you sent, with CPOL/CPHA read from CR1;
  5. one individual variation, predicted before flashing.
